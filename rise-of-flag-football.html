Flag Football Is Having a Moment — Here's Why That Matters for Your Kid

August 2026  ·  Justin Floyd

Flag football used to be the thing kids played at recess before "real" football started. That's not true anymore, and the shift happened fast.

Girls flag football is becoming a real varsity sport

Over the last few years, girls flag football has gone from a handful of school districts running pilot programs to an officially sanctioned high school varsity sport in a growing list of states, including California, Nevada, Alabama, Florida, Georgia, and New York, with more states adding it through their athletic associations each year. That sanctioning matters because it means scholarships, letterman jackets, state championships, and a real athletic pathway for girls that mostly didn't exist a decade ago. The NFL has backed this push directly, partnering with state athletic associations and school districts to fund equipment, officiating, and coaching so the sport can actually launch instead of just getting approved on paper.

The NFL has bet big on flag, and the exposure follows

The NFL FLAG youth league has grown into one of the largest youth football programs in the country, and the league has leaned into it at the highest level. Flag football has been part of the Pro Bowl Games skills competition, giving it a national broadcast audience it never had before. The NFL has also been public about wanting flag football to become the sport's most accessible entry point for kids who might never play tackle. And flag football is headed to the 2028 Los Angeles Olympics, which is about as large a media platform as a sport can get — that alone has driven a wave of new coverage, sponsorship interest, and parent attention that didn't exist when flag was seen as just a backyard game.

Why more parents are choosing flag over tackle

The biggest reason parents give for choosing flag over tackle at the youth level is head impact exposure. Flag football removes blocking and tackling, which means it removes most of the repeated head contact that's driven so much of the medical concern around youth tackle football in recent years. Multiple youth sports medicine organizations have pointed to flag as a lower-risk way for kids to learn the actual game — routes, coverage, footwork, game awareness — without absorbing the physical toll of full contact at a young age. Kids still get real competition, real coaching, and a real path to tackle later if that's what they want. They just don't have to start there.

What this means for Fathers and Football

This is exactly the wave FAF is part of. Frisco Elite is playing in a sport that's getting bigger, safer to enter, and more visible every year — real varsity pathways for girls, real national exposure through the NFL, and a real answer for parents who want their kids playing real football without full contact. That's the case for flag, and it's the case for why we do what we do.
